Embedded Finance: What Happens When the Bank Becomes Invisible?

In embedded finance the bank leaves the interface, but where does responsibility go? Roles, the Synapse case, Turkey’s rules and what must stay visible.

6 min read·Türkçe ↗

My short answer: The bank can step out of the interface, but not out of responsibility. The real question in embedded finance is this: When users no longer see the bank, what does their trust rest on?

In the first issue of AFTER MONEY, I asked whether AI agents will replace banking apps. Here I come at the same theme from another side: What happens when banking leaves its own app and moves inside other brands? As before, I will keep evidence, interpretation and forecast apart.

What is embedded finance?

A 2024 report by the Euro Banking Association (EBA; not to be confused with the European Banking Authority) describes embedded finance as placing financial services such as payments, lending, insurance or investments inside mostly non-financial platforms. Users do not have to leave the platform to use them.

The same report treats Banking-as-a-Service (BaaS) in two ways: as an infrastructure stack of APIs, compliance and security layers, and as a business model that lets unlicensed companies offer regulated services under their own brand.

Who sees what?

The EBA report describes four roles in this setup. The fact that users usually see only one of them is what this piece is about:

RoleWhat it doesDoes the user see it?
Licensed providerA bank, payment or e-money institution. Provides the regulated service and the balance sheet; handles compliance, customer due diligence and risk.Usually not
EnablerThe middleware connecting the licensed provider to the platform. Manages APIs and integration.No
DistributorThe platform that owns the interface. Markets the product and handles customer support.Yes
ConsumerUses the service inside the platform.—

The roles come from the EBA report; the last column is my interpretation.

Apple Card is an everyday example. The card carries Apple’s brand, and the bank behind it has been Goldman Sachs since its 2019 launch. In January 2026, it was announced that JPMorgan will take over that role. According to Apple, nothing will change for users and cards can be used as normal; the deal is expected to close in about two years. In other words, the bank behind a card can change without users noticing.

The same logic applies to businesses. Stripe offers financing to businesses that use its platform. According to Stripe’s documentation, that financing comes from different financial partners, while the offers are viewed and accepted through Stripe.

Why it appeals

The logic is simple: Financial needs usually arise in the middle of something else. At checkout, in a merchant’s sales dashboard, inside an app. The platform reaches the user at exactly that moment; the licensed institution gains distribution it could not reach through its own channels.

The EBA report also notes the cost: Platforms with direct access to users may take over the customer relationship. It stresses that responsibility and liability between partners need to be divided clearly.

When things break: the Synapse case

The clearest public example of what can happen when the bank is invisible comes from the United States. Synapse was a middleware company connecting fintech apps to partner banks. It filed for bankruptcy on 22 April 2024.

According to TechCrunch, citing the company’s filings, as many as 100 fintechs and 10 million end users were potentially affected by the end of May 2024. In July 2024, about $158.6 million owed to end users was still outstanding, and the shortfall was estimated at $65 million to $95 million.

At its core, the problem was record-keeping. In October 2024, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) published a proposed rule. Under it, banks holding certain accounts with fintech users’ money would record each beneficial owner and their balance, reconcile those records at least daily, and, if a third party keeps the records, have direct access to them even if that party goes bankrupt. The FDIC cited Synapse, where users lost access to their money for months.

In August 2025, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) sued Synapse. It alleged that the company failed to track consumer funds properly and failed to make sure its records matched those of its partner banks.

The lesson, as I see it: When the bank is invisible, the most critical question becomes “Who holds the record of my money, and is it right?”

The framework in Turkey

Turkey has a legal basis for this model. The BDDK (Banking Regulation and Supervision Agency) regulation on digital banks and service model banking was published in the Official Gazette on 29 December 2021 and took effect on 1 January 2022. According to the BDDK, one of its aims is to set the conditions under which banking services can be offered to fintech companies and other businesses as a service model.

One more distinction matters for users: the type of balance shown in an app. Under Turkish law, funds received in exchange for e-money are not deposits and are not covered by the deposit insurance of the Savings Deposit Insurance Fund (TMSF). Instead, a safeguarding regime under Law No. 6493 applies: Funds are held in separate accounts at banks and, if the institution is wound up, are used first to satisfy the rights of the fund owners. So two balances that look the same on screen can fall under different protection regimes. This is not legal advice; check the relevant institutions’ own statements for details.

What must stay visible when the bank is invisible?

The bank can leave the interface. But in my view, four pieces of information should always be visible to users, whichever brand’s app they are in. These are product design proposals, not legal requirements:

  1. Who holds my money? The licensed institution providing the service should be named clearly at sign-up and in the account details.
  2. Which protection applies? Whether the balance is a deposit or e-money, and what protection follows from that, should be explained in plain words.
  3. Who do I turn to if something goes wrong? The complaint path and the responsible party should be findable on a single screen.
  4. Where is the authoritative record? Users should be able to get an account statement that the licensed institution can also confirm.

The Synapse case shows why the fourth point matters: The crisis became a question of whose records were right.

My scenario: the bank becomes infrastructure

From here on, this is a forecast. In my scenario, a growing share of banking services is offered inside other brands, and the bank exists less as a logo and more as infrastructure. Trust moves from the logo to information about control and transparency.

Combined with the agent scenario from the first issue, the picture gets interesting: The user instructs an agent, the agent acts on a platform, and a bank stands behind the platform. The user ends up two steps away from the bank. That is why the easier it is to find the answer to “Who is responsible?” in the interface, the sturdier the model will be.

There are ways this could prove wrong. Regulators may require the licensed institution to be more visible. Large platforms may get their own licenses and cut out the middleware layer. Or, after cases like Synapse, users may want to see their bank again. No one can say with data today which of these will win out.
