Are Digital Wallets Becoming the Home Screen of Finance?

A wallet’s strategic value may depend as much on the decisions people make inside it as on the number of cards it holds.

30 October 2026·5 min read·Türkçe ↗

My short answer: Wallets are taking up more and more of the payment moment, but becoming the home screen of finance is something else. That position will go not to the app people open most often, but to the one they trust when something goes wrong.

At the end of a purchase, a wallet opens on your phone. You choose a payment method, approve the transaction and move on. Your bank is working behind the experience, but the screen you see at that moment may belong to another company.

The question here is not whether all of banking will end up in a single wallet. The real question is how much of a role a wallet can earn in people’s financial decisions, and how it can earn that position.

First, which wallet are we talking about?

Three questions help when assessing products: Does the app manage payment credentials, offer a spendable balance, or help people see several financial relationships in one place? These are functional distinctions I use for this piece, not legal categories; one product can combine several of them.

Apple, for example, says that when you pay with Apple Pay in apps or on the web, your actual card number is not shared with the merchant. That changes how an existing payment instrument is used, but on its own it does not turn the wallet into a bank account.

So seeing a card or a balance on screen does not tell you who holds the money, who provides the service or who is responsible when something goes wrong. A good product should let people find those answers without getting lost between several contracts.

Why payment frequency is strategic

An app tied to an everyday need earns a regular place in someone’s life. For a wallet, the payment moment is the natural start of that relationship. But being opened often and being trusted with financial decisions are not the same measure of success.

Someone may prefer a wallet at checkout while still tracking savings with their bank and making credit decisions through another channel. In my view, a wallet’s claim to be the “home screen” depends on building a meaningful link between those separate needs.

Showing a transaction is easy. Helping someone understand why it appears twice, dispute a wrong entry and follow the response requires a deeper service relationship. The price of sitting at the center is taking on more of the questions.

As convenience grows, so does the competition question

In the UK, a joint assessment published by the FCA and the PSR on 19 February 2025 found that the share of card transactions made through digital wallets rose from 8% in 2019 to 29% in 2023. It recorded benefits in convenience, security and, for some people, financial inclusion, while stressing that competition between wallet providers needs to improve and that new players need to be able to enter the market. These are UK findings; they do not show that every country has the same market structure.

The product question I draw from this: Who decides which choices a wallet presents? How the default card is chosen, where an alternative payment method appears and how easily people can change their preference are design decisions, and they can affect a service’s visibility. But without measuring how much those decisions change behavior, it would be wrong to infer automatic market dominance from the interface.

A wallet suggesting a payment method with AI is a plausible future scenario. In assessing such a product, I would look not at the speed of the suggestion, but at whether its reasoning is explained, whether commercial relationships are visible and whether people can easily choose another option.

A concrete infrastructure signal in Turkey

On 17 March 2026, Turkey’s central bank (TCMB) announced new open banking features. With version 2.0.0 of its Payment Services Data Sharing Services (ÖHVPS), card information and transactions were added to account information services, and future-dated and recurring payment initiation services went live. According to the TCMB, the ecosystem has reached 16.4 million users and 53 participants.

That is an important infrastructure step for the idea of bringing financial information and some payment functions together in different experiences. But it does not mean any wallet can access every account or card; each product’s scope, integration and authorization still need to be examined.

Think of the opportunity as a screen: an experience that helps people understand upcoming payments across institutions, shows where each piece of information comes from and offers control before any action. This is a product proposal, not a claim that such a service is available to everyone today.

What it takes to be the home screen

Judging wallets by downloads or the number of cards added is incomplete. The measures I propose are closer to the user’s task: Can people complete what they came to do, understand their options and take back control when something goes wrong?

From here on, this is a forecast. Digital wallets settling at the center of finance is one possible future; separate interfaces for payments, savings and borrowing all staying important is another. The available sources do not name a single winner.

I think a lasting advantage will come not from fitting every service onto one screen, but from building an experience people can trust again and again. A product that wants to be the home screen of financial life has to design clear responsibility, not just easy payments.
