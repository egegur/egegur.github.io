How Will Banks Make Money in the Future?

As customer relationships spread across wallets and AI interfaces, banking revenues need to be considered alongside the cost of delivering the service.

5 min read·Türkçe ↗

My short answer: Even as the interface changes, the economic foundations of banking stay largely the same: the balance sheet, paid services, distribution and operations. What changes is the channel through which money is made, and at what cost. That is why “How many people open the app?” is not enough to understand future revenue.

Imagine a future in which you open your banking app less often. You start payments from a wallet and review your financial information in another interface. The bank’s screen has moved into the background. Has the economic value of your account, your loan and the services behind your payments disappeared with it?

In this piece, instead of looking for a single new revenue source, I look at four areas together: the balance sheet, paid services, distribution and operations. This is not a forecast of industry profits; it is a framework I use to assess different business models.

1. Balance-sheet economics still matter

According to the Bank of England, banks’ core jobs are keeping money safe, lending and helping people make payments. Banks earn the difference between the interest they charge on loans and the interest they pay on savings; they charge more for riskier loans and can lose money when loans are not repaid.

Replacing an app with a smarter interface does not remove those economic relationships. However smooth the experience becomes, the cost of funding and the risk of the loans made are still there.

So when assessing the bank of the future, “How many people open the app?” needs other questions beside it: Which customer need is being met? What are the revenues and costs of that relationship? How resilient is the model when risk changes? An excellent user experience cannot, on its own, make up for a weak revenue model or mispriced risk.

2. Paid services must keep earning their place

According to Revolut’s 2025 results, announced on 24 March 2026, the company’s subscription revenue rose 67% to £708 million. The company also reports card payments, interest, wealth and foreign exchange as separate revenue lines. These are Revolut’s own group-level figures; they say nothing about the Turkish market.

The example shows that subscriptions can be a real revenue line in financial services. It does not show that they are the right model for every bank, or that subscription revenue means profit.

My product view: A package that charges a recurring fee has to deliver recurring value. A business, for example, might find it worthwhile to pay for a service that matches incoming payments with its accounting records, because it solves a concrete job. What matters is less the number of features in the package than whether customers actually use them. An offer that rests on campaigns or unused perks deserves a separate test of how durable it is.

3. Serving inside another product

A bank can reach customers through its own app. In another model, account or payment services are offered inside a different software experience, and the roles of the company running the interface and the institution providing the financial service separate. Commercial arrangements such as integration, usage or per-transaction fees are possible here; these are options I am discussing, not a common tariff used by banks.

The assessment should go beyond the number of customers reached: Who maintains the integration? Who fixes a wrong record? How does the customer’s service continue if the partnership ends? Every new distribution channel brings the cost of answering those questions.

In the US, a joint statement issued by the Federal Reserve, the FDIC and the OCC on 25 July 2024 reminded banks that offer deposit products through third parties of the risks these arrangements pose to safety and soundness, compliance and consumer protection. On the same day, the agencies also asked for public input on bank-fintech arrangements. This is a US supervisory statement; it should not be read as a rule of Turkish law.

My inference: A partnership that widens distribution is sustainable only if it also keeps responsibilities clear. Working invisibly does not justify blurring responsibility.

I looked at the user side of the same question, including the Synapse case, in AFTER MONEY 005.

4. Being digital does not mean one business model

An analysis in the European Central Bank’s May 2025 Financial Stability Review splits euro-area digital banks into two groups: those that turn the deposits they gather into loans, and those without material lending that hold deposits mainly in high-quality liquid assets such as central bank reserves. The same “digital bank” label can cover very different balance-sheet choices.

That distinction shows why surface similarity is a poor guide when assessing revenue. Two apps may look alike while their funding, risks and cost to serve are very different.

AI should be treated the same way. An assistant that prepares a transaction faster may be an efficiency opportunity. But seeing the net benefit requires counting model and infrastructure costs, human review, error correction and customer support together. This piece does not claim a measured AI saving at any bank. Revenue and efficiency also need separate measures: Cutting the cost of a task can create value, but it is not automatically a new revenue line.

My forecast

From here on, this is a forecast. A likely future for banks is a structure in which some services are delivered on their own screens, some on partner platforms and some inside more automated experiences. How revenue will be split across those channels cannot be pinned down today.

In my view, the sturdier question is this: Which need does the institution meet, which risk does it take on, and why would a customer or partner pay for the service? The strong business models of the future will come from those that can answer those three questions clearly. The visibility of the banking app may change; the need to explain where economic value comes from does not.
